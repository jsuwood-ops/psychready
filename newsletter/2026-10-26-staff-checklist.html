Issue 2 / Sign-off means someone saw it

A 10-minute huddle that turns a checked box into an observed skill using PsychReady's new printable staff training checklist, NIMH's open call for comment on rapid-acting psychiatric drugs, APA's Nobel news, and a quiet week at SAMHSA.

Welcome to Issue 2 of the PsychReady weekly brief: one practical idea for inpatient, residential, and clinic behavioral health teams, plus a short roundup of public updates from APA, SAMHSA, and NIMH.

This brief is for staff education and program planning. It is not clinical advice, and it does not replace accredited training, local policy, or professional judgment.

This week's training tip: sign-off means someone saw it

On many units, a competency gets checked off because someone finished a module or sat through a class. That tells you who was in the room, not who can do the skill on a hard shift. Here is a 10-minute huddle that turns one competency into an observed skill.

  1. Pick one row (1 minute). Open the new behavioral health staff training checklist and choose a single high-risk skill your team uses this week, for example "Offers at least two genuine choices" from the de-escalation table.
  2. Demonstrate (3 minutes). A preceptor or charge nurse runs a short role-play while everyone else watches for that one behavior and nothing else.
  3. Pair practice (4 minutes). In pairs, each person tries the skill once. The observer gives three-part feedback: what went well, what to change, and one next step.
  4. Be honest about sign-off (2 minutes). Mark the box only for people the preceptor actually saw do it. Anyone who wasn't observed gets a date for the next observation, not a check mark.

The checklist marks stay in your browser tab and are never sent to PsychReady, so print a copy if you need a record. Each row links to the matching free module, and the supervision module covers direct observation and when to withhold sign-off.

Research and guidance roundup

NIMH: public comment on rapid-acting psychiatric drugs, due November 9

On October 8, NIMH opened a request for information on how to build real-world evidence for rapid-acting psychotropic interventional drugs, which it calls RAPIDs. These are medicines that may reduce symptoms within hours or days and may need structured clinical delivery, monitoring, or follow-up. NIMH lists clinicians and health systems among the perspectives it wants to hear from. Responses are due November 9, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern. Read the notice: NOT-MH-26-090.

What it means on the floor: if your facility already delivers a treatment that needs monitoring after each dose, your staff know what that takes in practice, and this is a chance to say so.

APA: a Nobel Prize for optogenetics, and October journals

On October 6, the American Psychiatric Association congratulated psychiatrist Karl Deisseroth, M.D., Ph.D., a member of the editorial board of The American Journal of Psychiatry, on sharing the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for discoveries that led to optogenetics. The October issues of four APA journals, featuring research on neuromodulation, precision therapeutics, and esketamine, are also online. See the APA news releases.

Worth revisiting this week: APA joined nurses, emergency physicians, hospitals, social workers, and others in a September joint statement on workplace violence and protecting those who care. It is listed on the same news releases page and pairs well with a de-escalation refresher.

SAMHSA: no new releases since our last issue

SAMHSA's press room has not posted anything new since the October 2 STREETS announcement we covered last week. Check the SAMHSA press room for earlier 2026 grant awards, including the 988 Day funding announced in September.

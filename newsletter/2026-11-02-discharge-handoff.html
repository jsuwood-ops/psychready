Issue 3 / A discharge plan someone can actually follow

A 10-minute teach-back huddle for safer discharge handoffs, an NIMH-funded trial of suicide recovery support in crisis stabilization centers, SAMHSA's new NSDUH spotlight on adolescents, and new APA Publishing titles.

Welcome to Issue 3 of the PsychReady weekly brief: one practical idea for inpatient, residential, and clinic behavioral health teams, plus a short roundup of public updates from APA, SAMHSA, and NIMH.

This brief is for staff education and program planning. It is not clinical advice, and it does not replace accredited training, local policy, or professional judgment.

This week's training tip: a discharge plan someone can actually follow

The days right after someone leaves inpatient or crisis care are a well-known time of higher suicide risk. A discharge packet doesn't help much if the person can't say what's in it, or if nobody knows who calls first. Here is a 10-minute huddle to rehearse a safer handoff.

  1. Pick one recent discharge (1 minute). Choose a de-identified case from the past week. Keep names and details off the whiteboard.
  2. Teach-back (3 minutes). One staff member plays the person leaving. Another asks them to say, in their own words, their top two warning signs, one thing they'll do first, and who they'll call. If the "patient" can't answer, the plan was too long or too vague.
  3. Name the follow-up (3 minutes). Agree out loud who makes the first follow-up contact, by when, and what happens if nobody answers. Write it as one line in the plan, not a general "outpatient follow-up."
  4. Check the means-safety step (3 minutes). Confirm the safety plan says who is helping limit access to lethal means at home and that this was actually discussed with the person, and with family when they agreed to it.

The suicide risk and safety planning module covers safety plans and means-safety counseling, and the matching rows on the staff training checklist let a preceptor sign off only after watching the skill. In the U.S., anyone can call or text 988 at any hour; make sure it's written on every plan.

Research and guidance roundup

NIMH: a trial of suicide recovery support in crisis stabilization centers

On October 28, the University of Alabama at Birmingham announced that Jennifer Lockman, Ph.D., an assistant professor in its Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Neurobiology, received a $3.4 million NIMH grant for a five-year, multi-site randomized trial of THRIVE, a brief recovery-centered intervention for people after a suicidal crisis. It will be delivered by counselors and peer support specialists in group sessions with phone follow-up at crisis stabilization centers in Phoenix, Nashville, and Huntsville, and will look at whether it reduces care escalations and suicide attempts after discharge. Read UAB's announcement.

What it means on the floor: peer specialists and phone follow-up are being tested as part of the treatment itself, not an add-on. If your unit already does follow-up calls, now is a good time to check that someone owns them.

Reminder: NIMH's request for comment on rapid-acting psychiatric drugs (RAPIDs) closes November 9, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern. Clinicians and health systems are invited to respond. See NOT-MH-26-090.

SAMHSA: new data on religious beliefs and adolescent substance use

On October 28, SAMHSA released a National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) data spotlight using 2022 to 2025 survey data. In 2025, 68.4% of adolescents aged 12 to 17 agreed or strongly agreed that religious beliefs were very important in their lives, up from 60.8% in 2022. Alcohol use, nicotine vaping, and illicit drug use were lower among those adolescents. SAMHSA notes the findings don't show that religious beliefs reduce substance use. Read the SAMHSA announcement.

What it means on the floor: when you ask an adolescent what matters to them, faith and community may be part of the answer, and that can belong in a strengths-based plan.

APA: new books on positive psychiatry and adolescent interviews

On October 23, APA Publishing announced three new titles: Positive Psychiatry: A Clinical Handbook, Second Edition, on resilience, post-traumatic growth, and wisdom in clinical care, plus an adolescent version of the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (SCID-5-AMPD Module I-A) and its user's guide. See the APA announcement.
